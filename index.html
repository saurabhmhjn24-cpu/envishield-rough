1. Heat stress and “feels like” (Heat Index)

Temperature is converted to °F: F = 1.8T + 32. Initial estimate: H = 0.5[F + 61 + 1.2(F − 68) + 0.094R], where R is relative humidity (%). If (H + F)/2 ≥80°F, the Rothfusz regression is used:

H = −42.379 + 2.04901523F + 10.14333127R − 0.22475541FR − 0.00683783F² − 0.05481717R² + 0.00122874F²R + 0.00085282FR² − 0.00000199F²R².

For R<13% and F=80–112°F, subtract [(13−R)/4]√[(17−|F−95|)/17]. For R>85% and F=80–87°F, add [(R−85)/10][(87−F)/5]. Convert back with (H−32)×5/9. This is a screening estimate, not a measured body temperature.

Calculation basis: the displayed “feels like” temperature uses the US National Weather Service Rothfusz Heat Index regression (temperature + relative humidity). The green/yellow/red chart background retains this application's existing Safe/Caution/Danger boundary curves. The Safe→Caution boundary is T = 40.1247562 − 0.287219702RH + 0.00195590150RH² − 0.00000452294349RH³. The Caution→Danger boundary is T = min(42.5, 52.9675892 − 0.468153228RH + 0.00421088398RH² − 0.0000162471061RH³), with 42.5°C used below RH 29.137%. These boundary curves are local decision-aid equations in this application and are not official NWS alert thresholds. For occupational heat-stress control, NIOSH recommends WBGT for exposure limits; Heat Index is a screening alternative when WBGT is unavailable and does not account fully for workload, radiant heat or clothing/PPE.

References: [1] [2] [3] [4]

2. Wind chill calculation

Wind chill describes how cold exposed skin feels when the air is moving. The dashboard uses the North American wind chill equation of Osczevski and Bluestein, adopted by the US National Weather Service and Environment Canada.

Calculation basis: Wind chill = 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V0.16 + 0.3965TV0.16, where T is °C and V is wind speed in km/h. In this app it is applied at or below 10°C and above 4.8 km/h, matching the NWS definition range. Low-to-Extreme risk bands and approximate frostbite times follow Environment Canada; the >0°C “Minimal” band is an app display category.

Steps used by the dashboard:

  1. Read the air temperature T (°C) and the sustained wind speed V (km/h, measured at 10 m).
  2. If V is 4.8 km/h or less, or T is above 10°C, the equation is not used and the air temperature itself is taken as the effective temperature.
  3. Otherwise the equation gives the wind chill, which is rounded to a whole degree.
  4. The rounded value is placed in the Environment Canada risk band (table below). The band sets the cold stress chip, the advice and the chart colour.

On the wind chill chart the horizontal axis is wind speed and the vertical axis is air temperature. Each coloured band lies between exact curves on which the wind chill equals a band limit. The dashed line at 4.8 km/h marks where the equation begins to apply, so the bands step there. A reading is plotted at its air temperature and wind speed, and its band is the colour beneath the marker.

Environment Canada’s published chart covers winds up to 80 km/h and air temperatures down to −55°C; beyond that the value is extrapolated and flagged. In sustained winds above 50 km/h, frostbite can occur faster than the times shown. If wind speed is not entered, wind chill cannot be calculated and only the air-temperature minimum (“At least …”) is shown. Wind chill uses sustained wind; gusts are not substituted into it.

References: [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]

3. Indian Air Quality Index (AQI)

Each pollutant sub-index is calculated by linear interpolation between CPCB concentration breakpoints: I = I_low + (I_high − I_low) × (C − C_low)/(C_high − C_low). Overall AQI is the rounded maximum sub-index. At least three pollutants, including PM₂.₅ or PM₁₀, are required by this implementation; values above its tables are capped at 500.

Model estimates use 24-hour averages for PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂ and SO₂ (at least 18 hourly values), and 8-hour averages for O₃ and CO (at least six). CO is converted from µg/m³ to mg/m³. A configured CPCB service is preferred when a station within 25 km has a reading no older than three hours. Otherwise Open-Meteo modelled pollutants are used; the app estimates Indian AQI rather than displaying US AQI.

References: [11]

Pollutant detail. For a modelled estimate the app keeps the sub-index and level of each pollutant and names the one with the highest sub-index as the main pollutant; station-reported or typed-in AQI values carry no pollutant detail. The descriptions of the pollutants and the notes on masks follow WHO, US EPA and CDC guidance and are general information, not a diagnosis. [21] [22] [23]

4. UV Index, altitude and visibility

Live location readings request hourly UV Index and visibility from Open-Meteo, selecting the hour matching its current weather timestamp. UV and visibility are model estimates. Elevation is terrain height, converted to feet with metres × 3.28084; it is not a GPS altitude measurement.

Altitude is shown in metres and feet. UV categories follow the WHO scale: 0–2 Low, 3–5 Moderate, 6–7 High, 8–10 Very high, 11+ Extreme. Visibility below 1 km is flagged as fog-range visibility.

Visibility below 1 km is treated as fog-range (WMO definition) and raises a low-visibility warning in Recommended actions and in the Detailed health advisory. Visibility is a model estimate and is not listed in the Environmental overview.

The altitude-illness section uses the Lake Louise consensus definitions of AMS, HACE and HAPE, the 2018 Lake Louise AMS score and the Wilderness Medical Society 2024 guidance on first response.

Snowfall, snow depth and weather codes come from the Open-Meteo weather model for the location. Snow is treated as present on the ground from about 2 cm of modelled depth, and as falling when the model gives snowfall of 0.1 cm per hour or more, or a snow weather code. Fresh snow can reflect up to about 80% of UV radiation (some sources give up to 90%); the displayed UV Index is not adjusted for this, and a note is shown instead. Modelled snow is a coarse indicator, and avalanche risk is not assessed.

References: [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] [20]

Altitude is shown in metres and feet (feet = metres × 3.28084). The three “high altitude” stages (9,000–12,000 ft, 12,000–15,000 ft and above 15,000 ft) are a grouping used by this app to describe the altitude of a place; they are not Wilderness Medical Society risk categories, which are set by the pace of ascent and past illness.

5. Actions, clothing guidance and forecast timeline

The next 12 forecast hours use Open-Meteo temperature, humidity, sustained wind and UV data. Thermal categories use the same equations as the chart; automatic mode switches below 20°C. The AQI forecast applies the existing Indian AQI interpolation and rolling averages to hourly model pollutant forecasts; unavailable pollutants remain unavailable. Weather and AQI are separate model estimates.

Weather age uses the provider timestamp; retrieval time is shown in the update-time tooltip. Live weather older than 90 minutes is flagged in Recommended actions. A forecast validity time is not its model-run issue time. Manual values are not verified measurements.

Recommended actions use the displayed conditions, with higher-category hazards listed first. This ordering is a presentation choice, not a validated combined risk score. Clothing suggestions are general layering guidance; garment insulation, wetness, workload and individual factors prevent an exact clothing count or safe duration being inferred from air temperature.

References: [4] [8] [9] [10]

6. Data sources and limitations

Weather, UV, visibility, elevation and air-quality model data come from Open-Meteo.com (CC BY 4.0). The Shakurbasti weather station provides measured temperature and humidity and, when its sensors report, wind and UV; otherwise a modelled value for the station location is used and marked.

These extra parameters are not requested for selected historical/future times or manually entered weather values and may show unavailable. Automatic chart selection uses the wind chill chart below 20°C; this switch is an app display choice, not a medical threshold. Missing wind uses air temperature without wind-chill correction; missing humidity prevents heat assessment.

7. First-aid quick cards

The cards summarise the first minutes of care for heat illness, hypothermia, frostbite, snow blindness, carbon monoxide poisoning, altitude illness, lightning injury and exposure to smoke or air pollution. Each step follows the guidance named on its card. They are reminders for the first minutes and do not replace first-aid training, local medical protocols or a medical officer’s advice. [24] [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31]

A card is marked “Relevant now” and moved to the top when the readings on screen match its rule: heat illness for a Caution or Danger heat zone; hypothermia for a wind-chill band above Minimal; frostbite for Moderate or worse; snow blindness for snow on the ground; carbon monoxide for a wind-chill band above Minimal, snow falling or on the ground, or carbon monoxide as the main pollutant with an AQI above 100; altitude illness at 2,450 m or higher; lightning when the weather code is a thunderstorm; smoke and air pollution for an AQI above 200. These flags only help choose a card; any illness should be treated according to what is seen in the person.

8. Self-check tools

Self-check tools. Each tool turns published criteria into a short set of questions and shows a colour-coded result with actions. Heat illness: confusion, collapse, a seizure or a measured temperature of 40 °C or more is treated as possible heat stroke; heat exhaustion is suggested by several symptoms after heat exposure. Cold injury: hypothermia is staged from movement, shivering and alertness, as in the Wilderness Medical Society table; frostbite and trench foot are suggested from skin signs, and rewarming advice depends on whether refreezing can be prevented. Hydration: urine-colour bands follow the eight-colour Armstrong chart (1–3 hydrated, 4–5 mild, 6–8 more severe), a body-weight loss of 2% or more counts as dehydration, and drinking advice follows NIOSH and OSHA (a cup every 15–20 minutes, not more than about 1.4 litres an hour). Altitude ascent risk: the low, moderate and high groups and the limit of 500 m a day in sleeping altitude above 3,000 m, with a rest day every 3–4 days, follow the Wilderness Medical Society guideline for people who are not acclimatised. [24] [25] [26] [27] [31] [32] [3] [4] [33] [34] [35] [36]

The symptom counts used to separate “urgent” from “early” results (for example, three or more symptoms for heat exhaustion) and the weight-loss steps for hydration are conservative choices made by this app, not published cut-offs. Results are general guidance for screening and do not replace a medical officer’s assessment.

High-altitude guidance

High-altitude preventive guidance
Use this section when planning ascent to or stay at higher elevations.

Altitude stages

The three stages group altitude for display in this app. Altitude illness can begin from about 2,500 m (8,200 ft); the risk groups used in the Ascent-risk check come from the Wilderness Medical Society guideline.

1. Gradual ascent

  • Do not go too high too fast. Above 3,000 m (about 9,800 ft), keep the gain in sleeping altitude to no more than about 500 m (1,600 ft) per day.
  • Add a rest day (no gain in sleeping altitude) at least every 3–4 days, and allow an extra rest day for every 1,000 m (3,300 ft) of ascent.
  • A one-day ascent to above 2,800 m is moderate risk, and to above 3,500 m is high risk.
  • Do not ascend further if symptoms compatible with acute mountain sickness develop. Resume ascent only after symptoms have completely resolved; descend if symptoms persist or worsen.

2. Pace of ascent at a glance

SituationGuidance
Above 3,000 mRaise your sleeping altitude by no more than about 500 m per day.
Rest daysAt least one rest day every 3–4 days, and an extra rest day for every 1,000 m gained.
One-day ascentAbove 2,800 m is moderate risk; above 3,500 m is high risk.
Past altitude illnessA history of AMS, HACE or HAPE raises the risk group. Plan the ascent with a doctor.

These limits apply to people who are not yet acclimatised. Individuals differ, and fitness does not remove the risk.

3. Activity during early acclimatization

  • Keep activity light on arrival and increase exertion gradually.
  • Physical fitness does not eliminate the risk of altitude illness.

4. Hydration, nutrition and substances

  • Maintain normal hydration; cold, dry air increases insensible fluid loss. Drink regularly, but do not force excessive fluids.
  • Eat regular meals with adequate carbohydrate intake during ascent and acclimatization.
  • Avoid alcohol early in a stay at altitude, and avoid sedative or respiratory-depressant medicines unless specifically prescribed.

5. Cold and UV protection

  • Use layered, dry clothing and wind protection; wind can markedly increase heat loss.
  • Protect exposed skin, hands, feet, head and face as cold intensity increases.
  • UV exposure increases with elevation. Use protective clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, UV-protective eyewear and broad-spectrum sunscreen; strengthen protection when the UV Index is 3 or above.

6. When to postpone ascent or descend

  • Postpone ascent during an acute illness, especially significant respiratory or gastrointestinal illness.
  • Stop further ascent for new headache with nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue or sleep disturbance after gaining altitude.
  • Descend and seek urgent medical help for confusion, loss of coordination, severe breathlessness at rest, rapidly worsening symptoms or marked reduction in exercise tolerance.

7. Preventive medication

Medication to prevent altitude illness may be appropriate when rapid ascent is unavoidable or in people with a prior history of altitude illness. It should be considered after medical assessment rather than self-started.

Sources: ascent rate, rest days and risk groups from the Wilderness Medical Society guideline; general prevention principles cross-checked against CDC Yellow Book 2026. See References & methods.

Altitude illness: AMS, HACE and HAPE

Altitude illness is caused by too little oxygen after a recent gain in altitude. It ranges from mild acute mountain sickness (AMS) to life-threatening high-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE) and high-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE), which can occur together or separately. It usually occurs above about 2,500 m; treat compatible symptoms as altitude illness until proved otherwise.

2. Lake Louise AMS score (2018)

Use the score only after a recent gain in altitude. Score each symptom from 0 to 3: none, mild, moderate, severe or incapacitating.

Symptom0123
HeadacheNo headacheMild headacheModerate headacheSevere headache, incapacitating
Gastrointestinal symptomsGood appetitePoor appetite or nauseaModerate nausea or vomitingSevere nausea and vomiting, incapacitating
Fatigue or weaknessNot tired or weakMild fatigue or weaknessModerate fatigue or weaknessSevere fatigue or weakness, incapacitating
Dizziness or light-headednessNo dizzinessMild dizzinessModerate dizzinessSevere dizziness, incapacitating
  • AMS is present when there has been a recent ascent, the headache score is at least 1 and the total is at least 3 (maximum 12).
  • Suggested severity: mild 3–5, moderate 6–9, severe 10–12.
  • The score screens for AMS only. It does not replace clinical assessment and does not diagnose HACE or HAPE.

Sleep disturbance, scored in the older version, was dropped in 2018 because it reflects the altitude exposure itself more than AMS.

3. Lake Louise criteria for HACE and HAPE

HACE criteria

After a recent gain in altitude, HACE is present when there is a change in mental status and/or ataxia in a person with AMS, or both a change in mental status and ataxia in a person without AMS.

Ataxia is often the earliest sign. Check balance by asking the person to walk heel-to-toe along a straight line, and note confusion, drowsiness or unusual behaviour.

HAPE criteria

After a recent gain in altitude, HAPE is present when at least two symptoms and at least two signs are found.

Symptoms (at least two)

  • Breathlessness at rest
  • Cough
  • Weakness or reduced exercise performance
  • Chest tightness or congestion

Signs (at least two)

  • Crackles or wheeze in at least one lung field
  • Central cyanosis (blue lips or tongue)
  • Rapid breathing (tachypnoea)
  • Rapid pulse (tachycardia)

Early warning: breathlessness on exertion out of proportion to companions, a dry cough, reduced performance and slow recovery; later, breathlessness at rest and frothy or pink sputum.

HACE and HAPE can occur together, and HAPE can occur without AMS.

4. First response

  • Stop ascending at the first sign of AMS. Do not leave the person alone, and allow no further ascent until all symptoms have gone.
  • Mild AMS: rest at the same altitude, keep warm, drink fluids, avoid alcohol and exertion, and reassess regularly. Descend if symptoms persist or worsen.
  • Severe AMS, HACE or HAPE: start descent immediately (an assisted or carried descent for HACE and HAPE) and inform the medical officer. Descent is the single best treatment; do not delay it for medicines or to see whether symptoms improve.
  • Give oxygen if available (enough to bring SpO₂ above 90% or relieve symptoms) while arranging descent. If descent is not possible, use a portable hyperbaric chamber if one is available.
  • Descent of about 300–1,000 m (1,000–3,300 ft) usually brings improvement; continue until the person is clearly better.
  • Medicines such as acetazolamide, dexamethasone or nifedipine are for the medical officer to prescribe in line with local protocol. They supplement descent and never replace it.

5. Returning to altitude

Resume ascent only when all symptoms have completely resolved. After HACE or HAPE, resume only after medical assessment and clearance.

Sources: Lake Louise consensus definitions (1991) for HACE and HAPE; the 2018 Lake Louise AMS score; first-response principles from the Wilderness Medical Society 2024 guideline and CDC Yellow Book 2026.
